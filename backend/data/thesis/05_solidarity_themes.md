# Themes from non-Venezuelan solidarity activist interviews

Key ideas that permeate solidarity activist interviews:

1. **Solidarity with el pueblo** — Activists extend solidarity to Venezuela’s working class, imagined as supporting the government. The opposition is often cast as a white, wealthy elite unable to sympathise with brown working-class “people.”

2. **Combat media narratives** — Activists feel compelled to fight mainstream media portrayals via social media despite abuse, insults, and death threats online and offline.

3. **Anti-imperialism** — Firmly anti-American (while Chinese engagement may be seen positively), grounded in histories of US intervention in Latin America; sometimes extended to criticism of Britain.

4. **Loyalty to socialism** — Many feel loyalty to an attempted socialist project and see Venezuela as a large country rhetorically fighting neoliberalism. This includes admiration for Chávez-era social policies (literacy, barrio medicine, communal councils, worker-led factories).

5. **Racialised and class polarisation** — Some activists articulate sophisticated accounts of mestizo ideology and racism embedded in opposition factions.

6. **Grassroots revolution** — The Bolivarian process is often framed as struggle from within the Venezuelan people beyond leadership alone.

7. **Alternative media ecosystems** — Heavy use of RT, TeleSur, Venezuelan government sources, and venezuelanalysis.com.

8. **Historical literacy** — Unexpectedly detailed knowledge of recent Venezuelan history, including the 1989 anti-IMF Caracazo revolts.

9. **Sanctions as economic warfare** — Emphasis on opposing sanctions, releasing confiscated assets, and preventing US military intervention.

## Points of disagreement among activists

Activists diverge on: satisfaction with Maduro; whether crisis is due to internal mismanagement/corruption under Maduro (or earlier under Chávez); whether popular support for Maduro would hold if crisis continues; and whether the Bolivarian Revolution is a “true” socialist project.

Anti-imperialism is less divisive than enthusiasm for Maduro personally. After 2019, none of the interviewed participants supported US intervention or Juan Guaidó as interim president.
