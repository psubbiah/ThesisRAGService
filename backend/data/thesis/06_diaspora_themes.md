# Themes from Venezuelan diaspora interviews

## Why they left

- Early 2000s leavers often cite studies, work abroad, or fear around private property after Chávez’s election.
- Those leaving in the prior 5–10 years (relative to 2019) emphasise deterioration of middle-class life, crime/insecurity, and blocked professional/economic futures.
- Very recent leavers emphasise sending remittances, medicine shortages, food scarcity beyond basics, power shortages, and visible desperation (e.g. people searching garbage). Many emigrated to South American countries and work multiple jobs.

## View of the crisis

Frustration, anger, and impotence are common. Blame is usually placed on Chávez and/or Maduro. Interviewees use “humanitarian crisis” language and insist the severity should transcend left/right solidarity politics. Some describe the situation as “not normal.” A subset attributes problems to Venezuelan cultural/political habits (viveza criolla, weak civic conscience, corruption).

## Views on US military intervention

Most saw intervention as the only way out of a government they view as perpetuating the crisis. A minority feared unnecessary deaths or doubted purely humanitarian US motives.

## Reactions to international Maduro supporters

Consensus of bewilderment (“Are they crazy? Ill-informed?”). Some assume activists are paid propaganda; others see ideology but call it misguided. Common responses: they should live there, not just visit. Offence is often visceral, linked to deaths from lack of medicines and supplies. Sanctions are frequently seen as too recent to explain the depth of economic collapse.

## Shared ground and core differences

Both solidarity activists and diaspora Venezuelans recognise a severe economic and social crisis. Principal differences: where blame is placed (sanctions/imperialism vs. government), and whether Maduro’s government still holds legitimate support of “the people.” Both sides mobilise el pueblo to contest legitimacy.
