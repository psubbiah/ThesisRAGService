# Theoretical contributions

## Transnational activism

Transnational activism is a relatively small but important strand of social movement research. Cost-benefit and rational choice theories struggle to explain it: participants often gain little material benefit and may take reputational or physical risks. Venezuela solidarity is distinctive because the “recipient” of solidarity — the Bolivarian government — is highly contested, unlike many rights-based transnational causes.

Western contexts normalise Global South solidarity activism in ways diaspora Venezuelans may experience as illegitimate or hegemonic, even when activists frame themselves as defending Venezuelan sovereignty.

Supporting the Venezuelan Revolution is more divisive than supporting equality or generic human rights. Solidarity lineages connect to Cuba, Nicaragua, and the Latin American left; younger activists may be more focused on Venezuela today via social media than on earlier revolutionary histories.

## Imagined people and populism

An understanding of “the people” tied to communities that are imagined (not lived) is theoretically important for populism studies. Populist ideas travel beyond national boundaries through an imagined Venezuela, imagined pueblo, and imagined revolution. Venezuela stands for values within a socialist ideology and becomes a domestic political symbol in places like Spain (e.g. associated with debates around Podemos).

## Digital methods and computational social science

Combining ethnography/interviews with computational text analysis of published corpora can test and extend qualitative insights. Critiques of computational approaches note decontextualisation from tokenisation and missed power relations in discourse — which is why mixed methods are emphasised.
