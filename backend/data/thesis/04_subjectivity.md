# Subjectivity and positionality of the researcher

The researcher’s family — especially her mother and grandmother — were fervently against Hugo Chávez’s government. Growing up in Caracas with a brown, lower-middle-class neighbourhood background while attending school in a wealthy area made inequality and polarisation personally visible.

She recalls first hearing Chávez on the radio in 1998 at age 12 and finding his rhetoric powerful, then being rebuked at home (“He’s a golpista”). Over time she developed a strongly negative view of the Bolivarian government’s role in Venezuela’s crisis, which made the loyalty of Chávez supporters — amid hyperinflation, shortages, and mass migration — a research fascination.

Speaking with non-Venezuelan government supporters abroad led to the insight that differing political positions can be motivated by a similar love for the country, but that there are effectively two countries, two concepts of people, and two nations in play — explored through language and emotion.

The thesis asks whether discursive prioritisation of the poor is enough to elicit support when the poor and working class have also suffered severely from the crisis. For non-Venezuelan supporters, discourse and anti-imperialist framing appear powerful; whether the same holds inside Venezuela remains harder to observe given travel constraints at the time of research.
