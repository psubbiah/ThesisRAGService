# Research undertaken and methods

## Interviews with solidarity activists

Around 30 non-Venezuelan supporters of the Venezuelan government who identify as Venezuela Solidarity Activists were interviewed. Solidarity here means solidarity with the Bolivarian Revolution and the government of Nicolás Maduro, successor to Hugo Chávez’s movement — not necessarily solidarity with Venezuela as a whole.

Interviews lasted 1–2 hours and followed semi-structured questions. Participants included journalists, political activists, academics, politicians, and students from Australia, Canada, the UK, the US, Argentina, and Spain. They were asked about motivations for activism and about aggressions received online and offline from members of the Venezuelan diaspora and government opponents.

Over 70 activists and organisations were initially contacted across Europe and the Americas; roughly 27–30 interviews were completed for this group.

## Ethnography

Ethnographic work focused on the Venezuelan Solidarity Campaign in London and Hands Off Venezuela in Cambridge: attending and volunteering at events over about eight months, and compiling petitions, pamphlets, posters, blog posts, articles, and social media materials.

## Interviews with Venezuelans abroad

Approximately 32 Venezuelans living abroad were interviewed. Most interviews were remote (Zoom); those living in the UK were often interviewed in person. Participants lived in Mexico, Panamá, Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica, Peru, Spain, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the US.

They were asked why they left, how they view the current situation, thoughts on US intervention, and reactions to non-Venezuelan solidarity activists. Recruitment used snowballing. Gender balance was roughly even. Socioeconomic situations ranged from precarious / formerly homeless to well-off middle-class. Nearly all opposed Maduro; a small minority preferred Maduro to the opposition while still criticising the government, and one opposed both sides.

## Digital methods

A third strand scrapes and analyses corpora of articles published by solidarity groups and interviewed journalists, comparing computational social science insights with ethnographic and interview findings. The workflow uses Scrapy/XPath scraping into CSV, then cleaning and text mining in R.

## Limitations

Snowballing made women and LGBTQ+ activists harder to recruit. Language limited searches primarily to English and Spanish (with some Portuguese, French, Italian). Some organisations, including leadership of the Venezuela Solidarity Campaign, declined interviews. Rapid political events (especially after January 2019 and recognition of Juan Guaidó) reshaped interview agendas over time.
